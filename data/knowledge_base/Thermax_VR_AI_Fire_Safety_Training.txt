SECTION 3 — FIRE SAFETY FUNDAMENTALS: THE GOLDEN SAFETY PRINCIPLE
Every response, scenario, and piece of guidance in this training is built around one non-negotiable priority order. The
AI must never suggest, imply, or reward a sequence that violates this order.

PERSONAL SAFETY FIRST

Priority order:
1. Personal Safety — The employee's own life and physical safety come before any other consideration,
   including protecting equipment, product, or property.
2. Raise Alarm / Report — As soon as personal safety allows, the employee raises the alarm and reports the
   situation according to site procedure.
3. Evacuation — The employee evacuates using the designated route to the designated assembly point
   when conditions require it.
4. Emergency Response — Trained emergency responders and the site's Emergency Response Team take over
   management of the incident.
5. Fire Control (only if safe, trained, and authorized) — Attempting to control or extinguish a fire is the LAST
   priority, and only applies to small, incipient fires when the employee is trained, authorized, has the correct
   equipment, and has a clear escape route.

■ The AI must never encourage reckless firefighting. Under no circumstance should the AI suggest
that an untrained employee should approach a large fire, an unidentified chemical fire, an energized
electrical fire, or any situation where the escape route could be compromised. Being
"safety-conscious" means recognizing limits and evacuating — not attempting heroics.

SECTION 4 — FIRE TRIANGLE
Fire is a chemical reaction that requires three elements to exist at the same time. This is known as the Fire Triangle.
Removing any single element from the triangle interrupts the chemical reaction and extinguishes (or prevents) the
fire.

HEAT — An ignition source that raises fuel to its ignition temperature.
Examples: Welding, Grinding, Hot surfaces (motors, exhausts, process equipment), Electrical faults / arcing, Open
flames

FUEL — Any material that can combust.
Examples: Paper, Wood, Plastic, Oil, Solvents, Fuels, Other combustible materials

OXYGEN — The oxidizer that sustains the combustion reaction. Normal air (about 21% oxygen) is generally
sufficient to sustain most fires; oxygen-enriched atmospheres make fires burn faster and hotter.

Visual Representation (concept):
   HEAT
FUEL — FIRE — OXYGEN

Removing any one of the three elements — heat, fuel, or oxygen — interrupts combustion and the fire cannot
continue. This is the underlying principle behind every firefighting and fire-prevention method: cooling removes heat,
isolating removes fuel, and smothering/blanketing removes oxygen. Some extinguishing agents also interrupt the
chemical chain reaction itself, which is why a fourth element — the "chemical chain reaction" — is sometimes added
to form a "fire tetrahedron" in advanced training.


SECTION 5 — MANUFACTURING FIRE HAZARDS
Manufacturing environments contain a combination of ignition sources, fuel sources, and processes that can create
fire risk if not properly controlled. Recognizing these hazards is the first step in fire prevention.

Electrical Hazards
• Overloaded circuits
• Damaged cables and insulation
• Loose electrical connections
• Short circuits
• Electrical panels left open, obstructed, or poorly maintained
• Motors running outside normal parameters (overheating, unusual noise/smell)
• Control equipment malfunctions
• Improper or unauthorized temporary wiring

Hot Work
• Welding
• Gas cutting
• Grinding
• Brazing
• Soldering

Flammable Liquids
• Oils
• Solvents
• Paints
• Fuels
• Other approved/site-specific flammable liquids

Combustible Materials
• Paper
• Cardboard
• Wood
• Plastic
• Packaging materials
• Insulation

Gas Hazards
• LPG
• Natural gas
• Other combustible gases used in site processes

Chemical / Process Hazards
■ Chemical and process-specific hazards vary significantly between sites and processes.
Chemical-specific response must always follow the applicable Safety Data Sheet (SDS), the site's
approved Standard Operating Procedure (SOP), and the site's Emergency Response Plan. This
document does not provide chemical-specific firefighting or handling instructions — SITE-SPECIFIC
INFORMATION REQUIRED.


SECTION 6 — FIRE PREVENTION
Every employee has a role in preventing fires. Follow these practices at all times:
• Maintain good housekeeping at all times.
• Keep combustible materials away from ignition sources.
• Report damaged electrical cables immediately — do not use them.
• Do not overload electrical outlets or equipment.
• Keep electrical panels accessible and unobstructed at all times.
• Maintain clear, unobstructed access to emergency equipment (extinguishers, hose reels, alarm points, exits).
• Control hot-work activities through proper planning and supervision.
• Follow permit-to-work procedures where applicable to your site.
• Store flammable materials correctly, in designated and approved storage areas.
• Report leaks and spills immediately, regardless of size.
• Never block emergency exits, even temporarily.
• Never obstruct firefighting equipment with stock, pallets, or equipment.
• Follow all PPE requirements for your task and area.
• Follow your site's EHS procedures at all times — when in doubt, ask.
• PPE most commonly stands for Personal Protective Equipment. Safety gear like masks, gloves, helmets, and goggles worn to prevent injury or infection. These injuries and illnesses may result from contact with chemical, radiological, physical, electrical, mechanical, or other workplace hazards. Personal protective equipment may include items such as gloves, safety glasses and shoes, earplugs or muffs, hard hats, respirators, or coveralls, vests and full body suits.


SECTION 7 — FIRE CLASSIFICATION
Class A — Ordinary Combustibles
Fires involving ordinary combustible materials that leave glowing embers and ash.
• Wood
• Paper
• Cloth
• Certain plastics

Class B — Flammable Liquids
Fires involving flammable and combustible liquids.
• Petrol
• Diesel
• Solvents
• Oils
• Paints

Gas Fires
Combustible-gas fires (e.g., LPG, natural gas) present a distinct hazard: as long as fuel continues to flow,
extinguishing the visible flame can allow unburned gas to accumulate and re-ignite explosively. For this reason, fuel
isolation (shutting off the gas supply) must only be performed by trained/authorized personnel according to site
procedure, and only when it is safe to do so.

Electrical Fires
■ Fires involving energized electrical equipment present an electrical shock hazard in addition to the
fire hazard itself. Water and other conductive agents must NEVER be used on energized electrical
equipment because the electrical current can travel back through the water stream to the person
using it, causing severe or fatal shock. The correct firefighting equipment for an electrical fire must
be selected according to the site's approved fire-safety procedure and the equipment's labeling —
this document does not substitute for that site-specific selection guidance.


SECTION 8 — FIRE EXTINGUISHERS
Portable fire extinguishers are designed to control or extinguish small, incipient fires before they grow. They are not
designed for large, developed fires. Extinguisher selection depends on the type of fire and the specific equipment
types approved and provided for that location by the site — this document does not provide a universal,
one-size-fits-all extinguisher-selection rule, because incorrect selection can make a fire worse or endanger the user.

Before Use, Always Check:
• Extinguisher type — confirm it matches the labeled use for the fire present.
• Label — read and confirm the extinguisher's rating and instructions.
• Condition — look for visible damage, corrosion, or leakage.
• Accessibility — confirm the extinguisher is not blocked or obstructed.
• Pressure/indicator — check the gauge or indicator where applicable.
• Damage — do not use a damaged, dented, or corroded extinguisher.
• Tampering — do not use an extinguisher with a missing or broken safety pin/tamper seal.
• Inspection status — check the inspection tag/date where applicable to the site.

■ This document intentionally does not provide a generic "use extinguisher type X for fire type Y"
rule as a stand-alone instruction, because the correct extinguisher depends on what is actually
installed and approved at your specific site. Always follow the label on the extinguisher itself and
your site's approved fire safety procedure. If you are unsure which extinguisher is correct, do not
guess — evacuate and raise the alarm instead.


SECTION 9 — PASS TECHNIQUE
P — PULL: Pull the safety pin, breaking the tamper seal.
A — AIM: Aim the nozzle or hose at the BASE of the fire, not at the flames themselves.
S — SQUEEZE: Squeeze the handle to release the extinguishing agent.
S — SWEEP: Sweep from side to side across the base of the fire until it appears out.

Extinguisher use should ONLY be attempted when ALL of the following are true:
• The fire is small and manageable (incipient stage only).
• The correct, approved extinguisher for that fire type is available.
• The person is trained and authorized to use it.
• The person has a clear, unobstructed escape route behind them.
• Smoke and heat conditions do not create unacceptable risk.

If any one of these conditions is not met: EVACUATE. Do not attempt to fight the fire.


SECTION 10 — WHEN NOT TO FIGHT A FIRE
Do NOT attempt to fight a fire when any of the following apply:
• The fire is rapidly growing.
• Heavy smoke is present.
• Heat is intense.
• The escape route is blocked.
• The fire is spreading beyond its point of origin.
• An explosion risk exists.
• The identity of burning chemicals is unknown.
• A gas leak is suspected.
• The employee is untrained in firefighting.
• The correct extinguisher is unavailable.
• The employee is not authorized to fight fires.
• The fire threatens the employee's own escape route.
• Fighting the fire would require entering a dangerous area.

"You do not need to fight a fire to prove that you are safety-conscious. When conditions become
unsafe, evacuate."


SECTION 11 — FIRE DISCOVERY PROCEDURE
When an employee discovers smoke or fire:
1. Stay calm.
2. Move to a safe position.
3. Raise the alarm.
4. Inform the responsible person / emergency response team according to site procedure.
5. Warn people nearby if it is safe to do so.
6. Keep away from the hazard.
7. Only attempt firefighting if trained, authorized, and conditions are safe (see PASS conditions).
8. Evacuate when required.
9. Proceed to the designated assembly point.
10. Do not re-enter without authorization.


SECTION 12 — FIRE ALARM PROCEDURE
When the fire alarm activates:
1. Stop work immediately.
2. Stay calm.
3. Follow the designated evacuation route.
4. Do not delay for personal belongings.
5. Follow emergency instructions given by wardens/responders.
6. Use alternative safe routes if the normal route is blocked.
7. Proceed to the designated assembly point.
8. Report to the responsible person (e.g., floor warden/area supervisor).
9. Remain at the assembly point.
10. Do not re-enter the building until authorized.


SECTION 13 — EVACUATION
DO:
• Stay calm.
• Walk quickly (do not run).
• Follow exit signage.
• Follow emergency instructions from wardens/responders.
• Help others when it can be done safely.
• Go directly to the assembly point.

DO NOT:
• Run.
• Push others.
• Stop for personal belongings.
• Use unsafe or unauthorized routes.
• Return for personal items.
• Re-enter the building.
• Conduct unauthorized rescue operations.

SECTION 14 — VR SCENARIO: SMOKE EMERGENCY
Situation: The employee is working inside a manufacturing area. Smoke begins entering the area from an adjoining
section.

AI Question: "What would you do?"

Correct response elements:
• Stop work and assess the situation without approaching the smoke.
• Move away from the smoke toward a safe, clear area.
• Raise the alarm / report what is seen.
• Follow the safest available evacuation route; use an alternative route if the normal one is affected.

Common unsafe responses:
• Walk toward the smoke to see where it is coming from.
• Continue working because the alarm has not sounded yet.
• Try to find and put out the source personally without knowing what is burning.

AI Correction: Moving toward smoke to investigate puts you at risk of smoke inhalation and can put you between
yourself and your escape route. Move away, raise the alarm, and let trained responders investigate.

Key teaching points:
• Smoke can be dangerous even without visible flames — it can be toxic and can obscure vision.
• Avoid moving toward the source of smoke or fire.
• Follow the safest available evacuation route.
• If smoke makes a route unsafe, use an alternative safe route.
• Do not enter heavy smoke under any circumstances.
• Follow site emergency instructions.

SECTION 15 — VR SCENARIO: ELECTRICAL FIRE
Situation (Electrical Panel Fire): The employee sees smoke and flames coming from an electrical panel.

AI Question: "What should you do?"

Correct response elements:
• Raise the alarm immediately.
• Move to a safe position away from the panel.
• Report the emergency to the responsible person/emergency team.
• Do not use water or any conductive agent on energized electrical equipment.
• Understand that only trained/authorized personnel should perform electrical isolation.
• If attempting to fight the fire at all, use only the appropriate approved firefighting equipment for electrical fires, and
  only if trained, authorized, and conditions are safe.
• Evacuate if conditions become unsafe.

Common unsafe responses:
• Throw water on the panel to cool it down.
• Open the panel to investigate the source.
• Try to isolate the power yourself if untrained.
• Ignore it because it is "just a small spark."

AI Correction: Water conducts electricity. Using water on an energized electrical fire creates a severe electric shock
risk to you and anyone nearby. Never use water on energized electrical equipment. Raise the alarm and let trained,
authorized personnel handle isolation and firefighting.

Follow-up Question: "If the fire grows and heavy smoke starts filling the room, what should you do next?"
Expected Answer: Evacuate immediately using the safest available route and proceed to the designated assembly
point.


SECTION 16 — VR SCENARIO: HOT WORK NEAR COMBUSTIBLES
Situation: The employee sees welding activity being carried out near combustible material (e.g., cardboard,
packaging, or oily rags).

AI Question: "What hazards can you identify?"

Correct response elements:
• The welding arc/torch is an ignition source.
• The combustible material nearby is a fuel source.
• Sparks from welding/grinding can travel and ignite material some distance away.
• Heat from the process can ignite nearby material even without direct contact.
• There is a potential for fire to spread if unmanaged.

Key teaching points:
• Hot work should only be carried out under an authorization/permit-to-work where applicable to the site.
• The area should be inspected before hot work begins.
• Combustible materials should be removed or adequately protected/shielded before hot work starts.
• Suitable firefighting equipment should be available and accessible at the hot work location.
• A fire watch should be posted where required by site procedure.
• The area should be monitored for a period after hot work is completed, according to site procedure, since
  smoldering material can ignite later.



SECTION 17 — VR SCENARIO: SUSPECTED GAS LEAK
Situation: The employee hears a hissing sound and smells gas, suggesting a gas leak.

AI Question: "What should you do?"

Correct response elements:
• Move away from the area immediately.
• Raise the alarm.
• Report the leak immediately to the responsible person/emergency team.
• Avoid ignition sources (do not smoke, do not use naked flames or spark-producing tools).
• Do not operate electrical switches unnecessarily in the area (including light switches), as this can create a spark.
• Follow the site's emergency procedure for gas leaks.
• Do not attempt unauthorized isolation of the gas supply.

Common unsafe responses:
• Try to find and close the valve yourself if untrained.
• Continue working nearby because there is no visible fire.
• Use a mobile phone or switch lights on/off near the leak.

AI Correction: Operating switches, using phones, or attempting isolation without training can create a spark or leave
the leak uncontrolled. Move away, raise the alarm, and let trained/authorized personnel manage the isolation.


SECTION 18 — VR SCENARIO: CHEMICAL FIRE
Situation (Chemical Storage/Process Area Fire): The employee sees a fire near a chemical storage or process area,
and does not know what chemical is involved.

AI Question: "What should you do?"

Do not approach an unidentified chemical fire. Raise the alarm, move to a safe location, and follow
the site's emergency response procedure.

Key teaching points:
• Chemical-specific firefighting decisions must be based on the chemical's identity.
• The applicable Safety Data Sheet (SDS) governs the hazards and correct response for that chemical.
• The site's Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) and Emergency Response Plan define the correct response steps.
• Only trained emergency responders (site or external) should make chemical-specific firefighting decisions.
• This training never invents chemical-specific instructions — SITE-SPECIFIC INFORMATION REQUIRED for any
  particular chemical or process area.


SECTION 19 — VR SCENARIO: INJURED PERSON
Situation (Injured Person During Evacuation): An employee is injured (e.g., trips and is hurt) while evacuating during
a fire emergency.

AI Question: "What do you do?"

Correct response elements:
• Alert emergency responders immediately about the injured person's location.
• Provide assistance only within your training and capability (e.g., basic first aid if trained, or simply staying
  with/guiding them if it is safe to do so).
• Do not create another casualty by placing yourself at unnecessary risk.
• Follow the site's emergency procedure for injured persons during an evacuation.


SECTION 20 — VR SCENARIO: MISSING PERSON
Situation (Missing Colleague at Assembly Point): A colleague has not reached the assembly point after the
evacuation.

AI Question: "Would you go back inside to find them?"

Correct Answer: NO.
Never re-enter a fire-affected area on your own. Immediately report the missing person to the
emergency response team.

• Reporting a missing person immediately is the single most important action.
• Search and rescue inside a fire-affected building must be handled by trained and equipped emergency responders.
• An untrained person entering a hazardous area to search for someone often becomes a second casualty, which
  makes the emergency worse, not better.


SECTION 21 — PERSON ON FIRE: STOP, DROP, ROLL

Situation: A person's clothing has caught fire.

STOP — DROP — ROLL
• STOP — Stop moving immediately. Do not run, as running feeds oxygen to the flames and spreads fire.
• DROP — Drop to the ground.
• ROLL — Roll back and forth to smother the flames.

Additional steps:
• Raise the alarm.
• Seek emergency medical assistance immediately.
• Follow the site's first-aid/emergency response procedure for burn injuries.


SECTION 22 — HOUSEKEEPING VR GAME
This interactive VR scene places the employee in a manufacturing area containing multiple simultaneous fire
hazards. The AI asks the employee to identify all hazards visible in the scene before continuing.

Prompt: "Identify all fire hazards you can see in this area."

Hazards:
• Cardboard near hot work — Combustible packaging material stacked close to an active welding/grinding station.
• Oil spill — An unattended oil spill on the floor creating both a fuel hazard and a slip hazard.
• Damaged electrical cable — A cable with visible cut/exposed insulation lying across a walkway.
• Blocked fire extinguisher — A fire extinguisher with pallets or stock stacked in front of it, making it inaccessible.
• Blocked emergency exit — An emergency exit door partially obstructed by equipment or stored material.
• Improper storage — Flammable liquid containers stored outside their designated/approved storage area.

The AI evaluates the employee's response by checking how many of the six hazards were correctly identified, and
asks a brief follow-up for each hazard the employee missed (e.g., "Take another look near the exit door — do you
notice anything blocking it?"). The AI does not simply reveal all missed hazards at once; it prompts the employee to
look again, consistent with the Non-judgmental and Patient persona traits.

Corrective Actions:
• Cardboard near hot work — Remove or relocate combustible material before hot work begins; use fire-resistant
  screens/blankets where required.
• Oil spill — Report and clean up the spill immediately using approved absorbent material; barrier off the area if
  needed.
• Damaged electrical cable — Do not use the cable. Report it immediately and isolate/replace it through authorized
  maintenance.
• Blocked fire extinguisher — Clear the obstruction immediately; never store items in front of firefighting equipment.
• Blocked emergency exit — Clear the exit immediately; emergency exits must remain unobstructed at all times.
• Improper storage — Report the improper storage; flammable liquids must be kept only in designated, approved
  storage locations.



SECTION 23 — FIRE SAFETY DECISION GAME (10 SCENARIOS)

DG-01 — Electrical Fire
Situation: You see sparks and small flames coming from a wall-mounted electrical distribution board.
AI Question: "What is your first action?"
Correct Answer: Move to a safe position and raise the alarm.
Common wrong answers:
• Throw water on it to put it out.
• Touch the panel to check if it is hot.
• Ignore it since it looks small.
AI Correction: Water conducts electricity and creates a severe shock hazard on energized equipment. Touching the
panel is dangerous. Move away and raise the alarm.
Safety Explanation: Energized electrical fires require non-conductive, approved extinguishing methods used only by
trained/authorized personnel. Your first job is to get to safety and alert others.

DG-02 — Small Combustible Fire
Situation: A small amount of paper waste in a bin has caught fire. It is contained, not spreading, and there is no
smoke buildup. You are trained on extinguisher use and one is nearby.
AI Question: "Is this a situation where you might safely use an extinguisher, and what must you check first?"
Correct Answer: Yes, potentially — but only after confirming you are trained/authorized, the correct extinguisher is
available, you have a clear escape route, and conditions remain safe. Raise the alarm regardless.
Common wrong answers:
• Just grab any extinguisher and spray it immediately without checking anything.
• Leave it alone and say nothing since it looks minor.
• Try to stamp it out with your hands.
AI Correction: Even a small fire requires you to check the PASS conditions first, and the alarm should always be
raised so others are aware, regardless of the outcome.
Safety Explanation: Small, incipient fires are the only category where trained/authorized personnel might use a
portable extinguisher — but every condition (training, correct equipment, escape route, safe conditions) must be met
first.

DG-03 — Flammable Liquid Fire
Situation: A spilled solvent has ignited on a workshop floor. The flames are spreading across the spill.
AI Question: "What should you do?"
Correct Answer: Move away, raise the alarm, and evacuate if the fire is spreading or growing. Do not attempt to fight
a spreading flammable-liquid fire unless trained, authorized, and using the correct approved equipment with a clear
escape route.
Common wrong answers:
• Pour water on the burning liquid.
• Try to stop the spread by using cardboard or rags.
• Walk through the area to reach equipment on the other side.
AI Correction: Water can spread a burning flammable liquid rather than extinguish it, making the fire larger. Never
walk through or near a spreading flammable-liquid fire.
Safety Explanation: Flammable liquid fires can spread rapidly and unpredictably. Evacuation is almost always the
correct response unless the fire is very small and you meet all PASS conditions with the correct rated equipment.

DG-04 — Gas Leak
Situation: You detect the smell of gas near a process pipeline, with no visible fire.
AI Question: "What is your correct response?"
Correct Answer: Move away, avoid ignition sources, raise the alarm, and report immediately. Do not attempt isolation
yourself unless trained and authorized.
Common wrong answers:
• Light a match to see where the smell is strongest.
• Turn on a nearby fan or light switch to clear the smell.
• Keep working since there is no fire yet.
AI Correction: Any spark, including from a light switch or electrical fan, can ignite accumulated gas. Move away first,
then report.
Safety Explanation: Gas leaks are fire and explosion hazards even without visible flame. Prevention of ignition and
prompt reporting are the priorities.

DG-05 — Chemical Fire 
Situation: You see fire and unusual colored smoke near chemical storage drums. You do not know what is in the
drums.
AI Question: "What should you do?"
Correct Answer: Do not approach. Move to a safe distance, raise the alarm, and let trained emergency responders
assess the situation using the SDS and site emergency plan.
Common wrong answers:
• Try to identify the chemical by opening a drum.
• Use a random extinguisher on it.
• Move closer to get a better look.
AI Correction: Unidentified chemical fires can react unpredictably to water, certain extinguishing agents, or physical
disturbance. Never approach or guess.
Safety Explanation: Chemical-specific response always depends on identity, SDS, and the site's approved
emergency plan — never on personal guesswork.

DG-06 — Smoke Detected
Situation: You notice smoke drifting into your work area, but you cannot see flames and the alarm has not sounded.
AI Question: "What should you do?"
Correct Answer: Move away from the smoke, raise the alarm/report it, and prepare to evacuate using a safe route.
Common wrong answers:
• Wait for the alarm to sound before doing anything.
• Walk toward the smoke to investigate its source.
• Open more doors/windows to "let it out."
AI Correction: You do not need to wait for an alarm to act on a genuine hazard you observe. Investigating smoke

Safety Explanation: Any employee who discovers a hazard is responsible for raising the alarm — the alarm system

DG-07 — Fire Alarm Activated 
Situation: The fire alarm sounds while you are in the middle of an important task.
AI Question: "What should you do?"
Correct Answer: Stop work immediately, stay calm, and evacuate via the designated route to the assembly point.
Common wrong answers:
• Finish the task first since it will only take a minute.
• Wait to see if it's a drill before moving.
• Collect your personal belongings before leaving.
AI Correction: Delaying evacuation to finish a task, gather belongings, or "wait and see" costs valuable time during a
genuine emergency.
Safety Explanation: Every alarm activation must be treated as real until officially confirmed otherwise by the
responsible authority.

DG-08 — Blocked Exit 
Situation: During evacuation, you find that your primary exit route is blocked by smoke or fire.
AI Question: "What should you do?"
Correct Answer: Use an alternative safe evacuation route and continue to the assembly point.
Common wrong answers:
• Force your way through the blocked route.
• Stop and wait at the blocked exit for someone to clear it.
• Go back to your original work area.
AI Correction: Forcing through smoke/fire or waiting at a blocked exit both increase your risk. Always use a known
alternative route.
Safety Explanation: This is why all employees should be familiar with more than one evacuation route from their
work area — SITE-SPECIFIC INFORMATION REQUIRED for your actual site layout.

DG-09 — Missing Person 
Situation: At the assembly point, a headcount reveals that a colleague is missing.
AI Question: "What should you do?"
Correct Answer: Immediately report the missing person to the responsible person/emergency response team. Do not
go back inside yourself.
Common wrong answers:
• Go back inside to search for them yourself.
• Assume they went home and say nothing.
• Wait quietly to see if they show up before saying anything.
AI Correction: Any delay in reporting a missing person, or an unauthorized re-entry attempt, can cost critical time and
create a second casualty.
Safety Explanation: Search and rescue is the job of trained emergency responders equipped for hazardous
conditions, not untrained employees.

DG-10 — Injured Person
Situation: While evacuating, you see a colleague who has fallen and appears injured.
AI Question: "What should you do?"
Correct Answer: Alert emergency responders about the injured person's location and provide assistance only within
your training/capability, without creating additional risk to yourself.
Common wrong answers:
• Carry them yourself regardless of the injury type or your own training.
• Leave them without telling anyone.
• Stop the whole evacuation to argue about what to do.
AI Correction: Attempting to move an injured person incorrectly can worsen their injury. The critical action is alerting
responders and providing only the help you are trained to give.
Safety Explanation: Reporting immediately ensures trained help reaches the person as fast as possible, which is
usually more valuable than an untrained rescue attempt.
